One index, three layers.

adamthede.com links to almost nothing it publishes. This puts every place under the domain, and four elsewhere, in one list. It opens as a drawer from the top of the home page, stands at /index/ as a plain page, and closes the home page as a footer list. Nothing here is built. Round two, below the first five states, draws the drawer four more ways, each a map of routes from one fork. Adam chose F, the notebook tree, on 2026-11-01.

Round two: four map variants

The fork first, then the places, then the fan.

Each of the four draws the same 21 places as routes from one fork: open to anyone one way, family the other. Three rules hold in all four, so they compare on form alone. The family gate sits at the fork and carries the same two bars and the same words: the lock, Family, Sign in by email code, and the handwritten line. The email code is asked at the first family door a visitor opens, and every family place is named beyond the gate. Every route to a place is as wide or as long as that record is deep in years, read from a first year added to the data file. And a place whose first year no file states is drawn as unknown, with no width or length claim: dotted in C, D and F, and in E a question mark at today with no run.

In every variant the origin is the page you are on, A life, measured in seconds, which counts from 1910. Point at a place to light its route and read it out. Each desktop capture shows A Viewing Life pointed at, with its family edition lit.

06

Drawer C: the survey sheet

The drawer is a topographic sheet. Contour lines are years, one every ten, and the summit is the day of the survey. The road enters from the bottom margin at 1910, where you stand, and splits at a drawn junction. The family road crosses a dashed boundary at the gate. Each place sits on the contour of its first year, and each road is as wide as the record at its end is deep. The legend in the right margin is a plain list of all 21 and is the nav for anyone who will not read a map. Point at a place and a crosshair runs to the neat line, its own contour lights, and the collar reads it out.

Leans on
The Ordnance Survey and USGS quadrangle (the shelf's survey map: to scale, always, with the scale stated). Isotype for the two unsurveyed patches.
Before a click
The record is a hill, and most of it is high ground: nearly everything began after 1995, and the slopes below are bare except for the letters and the film you are standing in.
What it costs
Every place needs a hand-set bearing on the hill. Adding a 22nd place is a drawing decision, not a data entry.

07

Drawer D: the route diagram

A transit diagram, every line at 0 or 45 degrees. adamthede.com is the interchange, and you are standing in it. Two lines leave at 45 degrees: the open line climbs and fans into Read, Elsewhere and Look at the data, and the family line drops through the fare gate. The data line runs beside the family line, so a record and its family edition are one station drawn across both. Each segment is as heavy as the record at the next station is deep in years, so every line thins as it runs. On a phone the diagram becomes the strip map over a train door: the interchange, the fork, then every station in order, with the line's weight kept.

Leans on
The 1972 New York subway diagram and the shelf's rule from it: a system, applied everywhere. Minard for the weight of the line.
Before a click
Where the fork is, which door is gated, and that three family pages are the same stops as three public records, seen from the other line.
What it costs
Station slots are a grid. A new place takes the next slot on its line, but a seventh family page or a fourth pair forces a new row.
Open drawer D
08

Drawer E: the graphical timetable

A timetable sheet. Years run across, from 1995 to today, and every place is a line that leaves the home line in the year its record begins and arrives at its own row in the year it ends, or today. Open places leave upward, family places leave downward through the gate, which runs along the fork as the same two bars. Rows are set by first year, oldest farthest out, so the lines nest instead of tangling. Ended records stop short with a terminal tick. The film and the letters began before 1995 and enter from off the sheet.

Leans on
Marey's Paris to Lyon graphical timetable, the method Ibry drew first. The axis starts in 1995 because data.adamthede.com's own axis does, and because 17 of the 19 dated places begin after it. A 1910 axis would crowd them into the last quarter of the sheet.
Before a click
How long each record runs, which ones have ended, and that a record and its family edition leave home in the same year, as a V.
What it costs
Nothing is placed by hand: every coordinate is a year. It is the closest kin to the coverage map, so it has to stay a nav, with names on the left and no counts in the rows.
Open drawer E
09

Drawer F: the notebook tree (chosen)

A pencil tree on a notebook page, lettered by hand. The root is where you stand, and the trunk splits once at the fork. Each road is a limb and each place is a leaf at the end of a twig. A limb is as thick as the years of record above it, by Leonardo's rule: its cross-section is the sum of its twigs'. Where the two limbs meet, the three records and their family editions are twigs that touch. Point at a leaf and its card opens beside it. The page says nothing else but the date and the words I think. On a phone the tree runs down the page, and the family limb branches first, directly under the gate and its words.

Leans on
Darwin's 1837 notebook page with I think written above the first tree, and Leonardo's notebooks for the branching rule. The card is the influences shelf's card.
Before a click
That the open limb carries more years than the family limb, and where a public record and its family edition grow from the same moment.
What it costs
Hand lettering is slower to read than type, and the tree is the hardest form to keep legible as places are added. The pencil wobble is a filter, so it renders slightly differently in each browser.

What A and B do differently

  1. A is a printed index. Four groups run down columns, and every name, line and address shows at once with nothing to point at.
  2. B is a map. Every place is the same tile in one grid, and the group names sit in the grid as labels.
  3. B draws the sign-in wall. A double rule runs the full width, open places sit above it and family pages below. In A the family group carries the same lock and line in its own column.
  4. B has a readout. A strip on the drawer's bottom edge, the home page's meter idiom, shows the full address of the tile you point at, who can open it and where it opens.
  5. B pairs records with family editions. Point at A Viewing Life and its family edition lights up, joined by a dashed line through the wall. Two more pairs work the same way. A says this in each family entry's line instead.
  6. A is cheaper and calmer. It fits a 1280 by 800 window with a short scroll and needs no script beyond open and close. B needs about 60 more lines of script, and on a desktop its addresses show only in the readout.

Where the first years come from

  1. Two places are drawn as unknown: The anniversary pages and LinkedIn. No file states a first year for either.
  2. The letters archive starts in 1979, not 1910. letters.adamthede.com holds 47 years of letters, 1979 to 2025, per the reader's own README. The 1910 letters on the home page are a separate book and are not on that site. The film itself is the place that counts from 1910, so it is the origin in every variant.
  3. What I meant to read starts in 2011. Its row in the data site's manifest has its own span start, 2011.07, so it does not borrow Reading's 2005.
  4. The influences shelf starts in 2026. Its first year is the day it was built. Its subject runs from about 170 to 2020, which is not a span of this record.
  5. Elsewhere reads from thedetech.com's own timeline. Thede Technologies from the name's first use, 6 July 2011, and Tractor and Silo from silo's first commit, 22 February 2016. GitHub reads from the account's creation date, 30 June 2013.
  6. Three records have ended. The B1 Years, One Year of Office and Chicago River Elevations carry an until, and their depth is the years they cover, not the years since they began.

Decided here, with a recommendation each

  1. Depth. A record's depth is its last year, or today, minus its first. Recommend keeping it: an ended record should not look as deep as one still running.
  2. Width. In C and D a segment is as wide as the record it arrives at, and a shared trunk as wide as the deepest record beyond it. Recommend this over summing years, which would make the trunk wider than the sheet.
  3. The tree's limbs. F sums years by area instead: a limb's thickness squared is the sum of the years above it. Recommend it for F only, because a tree whose trunk is no thicker than its thickest twig does not read as a tree.
  4. Road order. Every road runs oldest first, so routes taper as they go. Recommend keeping it as the rule for any built version.
  5. E's axis. 1995 to today, with the film and the letters entering from off the sheet. Recommend it, for the reason in E's note above.
  6. The gate in E. The same two bars, laid along the fork, because family lines leave the home line in six different years. Recommend accepting it as the same mark.

# Proposed shape for data/index.yaml on adamthede.com (mockup, 2026-11-01).
# Not read by the site yet. The mockup pages in this folder were generated from it.
#
# One file feeds three layers: the drawer at the top of the home page,
# the plain page at /index/, and the list at the foot of the home page.
# File order is display order, with one exception: drawer variant B draws the
# family group below its sign-in wall, after the open groups. Variants C to F
# order each road by `since`, oldest first, because that is the order the
# routes taper in.
# Counts on the page are computed from this file.
#
# groups[]
#   id        stable key, used for anchors (/index/#family)
#   label     the heading a reader sees, named for what the reader wants
#   access    public | family. A family group renders the lock mark and the note
#   note      one line under the heading, optional
#   opens     here | new-tab. Elsewhere opens a new tab and carries the arrow
# entries[]
#   name      what the place is called
#   line      one plain line that says what the page is, never what is in it
#   url       the full address
#   id        optional stable key, needed only when another entry points at it
#   edition_of  family entries only, optional: the id of the public record this
#               page is the full family edition of (variant B lights the pair)
#   since     the first year the record covers, as a decimal year. Values copied
#             from data.adamthede.com keep its twelfths (September 2013 is 2013.7).
#             Values read from a dated source are that day's fraction of its year
#             (6 July 2011 is 2011.51). Every value is read from the source named
#             in its comment, never estimated.
#             `~` means no file states it, and every variant draws that place as
#             unknown: a dotted route, with no width claim.
#   until     only on a record that has ended: its last year, in the same form.
#             Without it a record is still being kept, and its depth runs to the
#             build date. Depth in years, (until or today) minus since, is what
#             variants C to F draw as the width or the length of a route.
